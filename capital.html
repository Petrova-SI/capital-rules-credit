01 / THE RULEBOOK

Capital is the bank’s shock absorber.

It is the part of a bank’s funding that can absorb losses before depositors and other creditors do. Capital rules ask banks to hold enough of it for the risks they take.

THE CORE IDEA≈ 4 MIN READ
A simplified capital adequacy ratio
Regulatory capital÷Risk-weighted assets=Capital ratio

Risk-weighted assets give more weight to exposures assessed as riskier. The ratio compares eligible capital with this adjusted measure of a bank’s assets.

↗

Why not just use total assets?
A low-risk exposure and a high-risk loan do not create the same potential loss. Risk weights try to reflect that difference.

THREE LAYERS

Minimum, buffers, headroom.

Regulation can combine a minimum ratio with additional buffers or bank-specific add-ons. A bank may be above the minimum but still have limited room before it reaches a buffer threshold.

WHAT CHANGES WHEN A RULE TIGHTENS?

The bank needs a larger cushion for a given level of risk.

A bank can respond in several ways: retain more earnings, issue eligible capital, reduce dividends, change the mix of assets, or grow more slowly in exposures that use more capital. The response can differ across banks.

That is why a higher requirement does not mechanically translate into an equal percentage fall in loans. Profitable banks with spare capital may meet a tighter rule without cutting lending. A bank close to its constraint may adjust faster.

Keep the measures distinct

Capital adequacy is about loss-absorbing resources relative to risk. Liquidity requirements address a bank’s ability to meet cash outflows. They can both affect lending, but through different channels.
